## Shared foundation and evidence rules

“Shared simulation” below means a proposed versioned event fabric over explicit state authorities, not merging their state by name. Monthly City, Population/House and aggregate Decision Studio housing have distinct clocks/IDs. Prefer one authority per prototype. Any cross-authority extension requires an explicit identity/time/unit adapter, defined missing mappings, ownership, and synchronization tests. New social rules require all seven ODD elements and inventory updates before implementation; none are added by this report.

Every prototype should retain model/rules versions, initial-state digest and source attribution, run/branch/parent IDs, scenario inputs, seed and random-stream scheme, interventions, units, exact time and evidence references. Repeated runs require immutable parents and matched inputs. New memory, belief, trust or preference mechanisms below are synthetic assumptions, never inferred human psychology. Distinguish recorded observations, synthetic premises, modeled outcomes and authored narrative. Keep absent events, departures, failed runs, unknown coordinates and incompatible versions visible. Matched comparisons estimate effects within the declared model; they do not identify real-world effects.

## Thirty proposed capabilities

### Emergent behavior

#### 1. Promise Cascades

- **What the user experiences:** You promise a neighbor a ride, then watch who must rearrange childcare, shifts and deliveries if you cancel.
- **Why it is surprising:** A small social obligation becomes a playable infrastructure dependency.
- **Required simulation data:** Population contacts, schedules and employment are foundations; promise deadlines, dependency edges, capacity and breach rules need explicit contracts.
- **What makes it functional:** Requests reserve actual time/capacity; broken promises propagate only along recorded dependencies, with a matched no-cancellation run.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** Use three synthetic households and one employer on one clock. Cancel one promise; inspect missed obligations and verify no effect on disconnected households.

#### 2. The Unwritten Queue

- **What the user experiences:** Residents invent a queue-sharing custom around a scarce service; you can join, defect or change opening hours.
- **Why it is surprising:** A social institution becomes something the player can discover rather than place from a menu.
- **Required simulation data:** Service arrivals/capacity plus new observable strategies, imitation, fairness and enforcement assumptions.
- **What makes it functional:** Strategies alter actual allocation; retain who learned from whom and compare against a fixed first-come rule.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** One service, twenty synthetic agents, two transparent strategies. Replay the same arrivals and show whether the custom persists after its founder leaves.

#### 3. Friendship Insurance

- **What the user experiences:** Build a mutual-aid circle and experience which households can absorb a job loss without losing their home.
- **Why it is surprising:** Relationships behave as a limited resilience resource with costs to helpers.
- **Required simulation data:** Household budgets and relationships; new consent, transfer, repayment and aid-capacity rules.
- **What makes it functional:** Every transfer balances both ledgers; aid cannot exceed resources or silently create money.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** Five households, one income shock, one capped aid rule. Compare matched runs and inspect both recipient protection and donor harm.

#### 4. Invisible Curfews

- **What the user experiences:** Walk a resident through a city where missed buses, care duties and shift times make certain opportunities effectively unreachable.
- **Why it is surprising:** The city has personal time boundaries even where roads are open.
- **Required simulation data:** Population travel and schedules; explicit opportunity windows, care obligations and feasibility constraints.
- **What makes it functional:** A time-expanded reachability calculation explains the exact missed connection, with unavailable routes marked.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** One resident, three destinations, one day. Change one bus departure and check whether the same job becomes feasible; no monthly City commute substitution.

### Counterfactual history

#### 5. The Last Reversible Minute

- **What the user experiences:** Scrub backward from a closure until the last checkpoint where a supported action can still prevent it.
- **Why it is surprising:** Time travel reveals a shrinking window of agency rather than merely another timeline.
- **Required simulation data:** Exact City checkpoints, business status, bounded interventions, completion times and costs.
- **What makes it functional:** Search replayed interventions at candidate months; report tested windows and nonmonotonic results without promising a global optimum.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** Test three intervention dates for one business outcome using the same parent and shocks; show failed as well as successful rescues.

#### 6. Undo the Rescue

- **What the user experiences:** After saving a neighborhood, remove your intervention and discover which gains endure without you.
- **Why it is surprising:** A successful policy can leave durable state or permanent dependence.
- **Required simulation data:** Checkpointed assets, households, policy lifecycle and explicit expiry/removal semantics, which need new support.
- **What makes it functional:** Run control, continuing-treatment and withdrawn-treatment arms; compare sunk assets, ongoing costs and outcomes separately.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** One supported temporary capacity mechanism with a defined expiry, three arms and one fixed horizon; prohibit generic undo of irreversible construction.

#### 7. History with an Alibi

- **What the user experiences:** Two players receive different plausible explanations of the same closure and must buy an experiment that distinguishes them.
- **Why it is surprising:** The winning move is discovering which observation would change your mind.
- **Required simulation data:** Competing versioned mechanisms, common observations, experiment menu and declared evidence costs.
- **What makes it functional:** Candidate mechanisms make different held-out predictions; scoring rewards discrimination, including an inconclusive result.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** Two synthetic mechanisms that fit one endpoint; reveal one withheld intermediate observation. Reuse Detective evidence handling, not its existing answer cases.

#### 8. The Counterfactual Heirloom

- **What the user experiences:** Give a future household an object whose journey records the moves and closures that almost separated its owners.
- **Why it is surprising:** Alternative histories become an intimate object biography.
- **Required simulation data:** Persistent household/place links and branch histories; object ownership/transfer rules are new synthetic state.
- **What makes it functional:** Every ownership transition references an actual event; cross-branch alignment uses common ancestry and marks missing owners.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** One object, two branches, five ownership events. Open each cited checkpoint; prevent narration from inventing an inheritance.

### Cities that remember

#### 9. Civic Amnesia

- **What the user experiences:** Erase an institution’s stored warning while keeping its staff and budget, then see whether a previous failure repeats.
- **Why it is surprising:** Memory is a manipulable input with consequences, not a decorative archive.
- **Required simulation data:** Institution identity, retained warnings, decision access and a new versioned learning rule.
- **What makes it functional:** A memory ablation changes only accessible information; decisions record whether the warning was used.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** One synthetic flood-response rule, two identical institutions with/without one warning. Show identical outcomes if the warning is irrelevant.

#### 10. The Address That Remembers

- **What the user experiences:** Stand at one address and see successive occupants disagree about whether its transformation was progress.
- **Why it is surprising:** A place has multiple legitimate biographies rather than a single citywide score.
- **Required simulation data:** Recorded occupancy, business changes and household outcomes; explicit preference functions and coverage.
- **What makes it functional:** Each viewpoint resolves to the same address/time evidence; preferences remain labeled synthetic interpretation.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** One building over three checkpoints, two affected households. Switch viewpoints and open exact rent/move records; no invented sentiment.

#### 11. Maintenance Archaeology

- **What the user experiences:** Dig through a street’s repair history to discover how today’s cheap shortcut created tomorrow’s expensive dependency.
- **Why it is surprising:** A budget decision leaves a physical memory that the player can excavate.
- **Required simulation data:** Asset condition, repairs, costs and explicit deterioration/failure rules; these are additional mechanisms.
- **What makes it functional:** Condition evolves from logged maintenance, not narrative tags; deferral shifts real modeled cost and service.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** One asset with a deterministic wear rule and two maintenance schedules. Compare lifecycle cost and outage duration from the same initial condition.

#### 12. Adopt a Forgotten Future

- **What the user experiences:** A returning player inherits an abandoned proposal and must learn why its author stopped before deciding to revive it.
- **Why it is surprising:** Saved experiments become unfinished civic stories with recoverable intent.
- **Required simulation data:** Existing recipes/ancestry plus opt-in author notes, objective revisions, permissions and compatibility checks.
- **What makes it functional:** Reopen only compatible immutable recipes; retain author intent separately from observed outcomes and expose missing versions.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** Import one local abandoned recipe with a note and failed objective. Rerun a supported revision; unsupported versions remain blocked.

### Across worlds

#### 13. Letters to Another Self

- **What the user experiences:** Write advice after experiencing one future, then decide whether your counterpart in another branch should follow it.
- **Why it is surprising:** The player can discover how locally sensible advice becomes harmful under changed circumstances.
- **Required simulation data:** Common-ancestor entity mapping, branch-local observations and a bounded advice-to-action contract.
- **What makes it functional:** Advice is an external player intervention, not magical agent knowledge; action acceptance and consequences are recorded.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** Two branches, one resident and one supported choice. Transfer a player-written note; compare obey/ignore under identical branch-local inputs.

#### 14. The Friendship That Never Happened

- **What the user experiences:** Meet two people who became friends in one branch and strangers in another; investigate the missed encounter.
- **Why it is surprising:** Absence becomes a traceable relationship story.
- **Required simulation data:** Population encounter records, relationship state, opportunity histories and ancestry-stable identities.
- **What makes it functional:** An encounter change uses explicit rules and matched streams; distinguish never-met from unrecorded contact.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** One scheduled encounter, two child runs, one rerouted trip. Show only relationship effects supported by the existing rule or label a new rule as experimental.

#### 15. Sister-City Exchange

- **What the user experiences:** Trade a constrained shipment between two synthetic cities and watch local solutions export costs to the other.
- **Why it is surprising:** A “successful” city policy can fail when its dependencies have a voice.
- **Required simulation data:** Explicit city boundary stocks, commodity units, transport delays and a new paired-city exchange adapter.
- **What makes it functional:** Double-entry stock transfers and synchronized clocks conserve goods; no inferred economic coupling from shared geography.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** Two tiny monthly cities, one commodity and one capped link. Compare isolation and exchange; test shortage, delay and conservation.

#### 16. The Reunion Room

- **What the user experiences:** Bring a family’s branch outcomes together around a table, then select whose trade-off you would accept.
- **Why it is surprising:** An ensemble becomes a difficult human choice instead of an average.
- **Required simulation data:** Existing household endpoint comparisons, same-ancestry mappings and explicit outcome dimensions; dialogue is authored interpretation.
- **What makes it functional:** Each seat opens its exact run; missing/departed members stay missing and no cross-world conversation is treated as simulated evidence.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** Reuse City Life outputs for one household and four paired outcomes; static cards first, no new agent mechanism or 3D room required.

### Scientific discovery

#### 17. Find the Rule That Fooled You

- **What the user experiences:** Make a prediction, inspect a surprising run, then design the smallest experiment that overturns your explanation.
- **Why it is surprising:** The city teaches scientific self-correction through play.
- **Required simulation data:** Pinned mechanisms, preregistered predictions, experiment inputs and held-out seeds.
- **What makes it functional:** Score prediction error on unseen runs and retain failed hypotheses; no result generated merely to reward the player.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** One supported policy, two hypotheses and three fixed held-out seeds. Reuse the bounded run API; distinguish mechanism fitting from external validation.

#### 18. The Disagreement Expedition

- **What the user experiences:** Enter the same scenario under two model versions and travel to places where their predictions diverge most.
- **Why it is surprising:** Uncertainty becomes territory to explore.
- **Required simulation data:** Version-pinned engines, explicit entity/time/metric adapters and compatible common input fixtures.
- **What makes it functional:** Decompose observed disagreement into known contract changes where possible; report unmappable entities rather than aligning by array index.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** Two frozen housing mechanisms on one synthetic fixture, one comparable metric and an exact disagreement table; add a map only for verified locations.

#### 19. Fragility Treasure Hunt

- **What the user experiences:** Search for a policy that looks excellent on familiar seeds but collapses on a hidden challenge.
- **Why it is surprising:** A high score can be a clue that the player has overfit.
- **Required simulation data:** Training/held-out seed manifests, bounded shocks, policy budgets and failure predicates.
- **What makes it functional:** Freeze the plan before revealing challenge outcomes; include failed runs and report coverage, not just best performance.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** Three candidate plans, five disclosed seeds and five fixed hidden seeds. Reveal all results after submission; no claim of representative real-world risk.

#### 20. Experiment on Your Experimenter

- **What the user experiences:** Give a simulated planning office a limited view of its own city and observe how its experimental choices bias its conclusions.
- **Why it is surprising:** The city contains an investigator whose mistakes are inspectable mechanisms.
- **Required simulation data:** New bounded planner selection rule, observation budget, accessible measurements and prediction history.
- **What makes it functional:** Planner uses only its recorded information; an omniscient diagnostic remains separate and cannot leak into decisions.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** A deterministic planner chooses between two tests from a fixed menu. Compare biased sampling and full coverage on one known synthetic mechanism.

### Collective intelligence

#### 21. The Evidence Relay

- **What the user experiences:** Each teammate can inspect only one part of a failing city; solve it by passing verifiable evidence rather than commands.
- **Why it is surprising:** Cooperation depends on assembling a shared explanation.
- **Required simulation data:** Role-specific observation permissions, evidence IDs, shared run identity and ordered submissions.
- **What makes it functional:** Server enforces views and validates citations; shared discoveries unlock only supported actions, never unrecorded facts.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** Hot-seat prototype on one saved City run with three sealed evidence packets. Test whether a combined claim cites all necessary records before any networking.

#### 22. Design the Tie-Breaker

- **What the user experiences:** Players agree on how to choose a policy before learning which household each will represent.
- **Why it is surprising:** The multiplayer invention is a fair decision procedure rather than a winning policy.
- **Required simulation data:** Matched household outcomes, player-submitted preferences, frozen selection rules and identity assignment.
- **What makes it functional:** Apply the chosen rule mechanically; reveal winners, losses, ties and missing outcomes without equating agreement to public consent.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** Three players, three existing plans and randomized synthetic role reveal after rule commitment. Local hot-seat and exported decision receipt suffice.

#### 23. Prediction Commons

- **What the user experiences:** Pool independently submitted forecasts, then let the city reveal where consensus was confidently wrong.
- **Why it is surprising:** Collective intelligence is measured by correction rather than vote count.
- **Required simulation data:** Timestamped predictions, pinned run recipes, frozen aggregation and held-out outcome records.
- **What makes it functional:** Prevent post-reveal edits; evaluate calibration and error with explicit sample counts, not popularity.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** Five opt-in players predict one synthetic metric over ten hidden runs; compare individual and median errors. No real-world forecasting claim.

#### 24. The Minority Rescue

- **What the user experiences:** A team’s majority plan passes; one player must expose a reproducible harmed subgroup and propose a constrained repair.
- **Why it is surprising:** Dissent becomes a productive gameplay role.
- **Required simulation data:** Household-level paired outcomes, declared harm thresholds, subgroup definitions and supported plan revisions.
- **What makes it functional:** Freeze subgroup criteria before evaluation, report coverage and rerun the repair on held-out seeds; no invented demographics.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** Three plans, one existing income-defined group, one repair knob. Show aggregate improvement alongside any unresolved household harms.

### Unexpected gameplay

#### 25. Play the Missing Link

- **What the user experiences:** Become a temporary courier, carer or technician inside a cascading failure and choose where your limited day matters most.
- **Why it is surprising:** A player is a scarce resource within a simulation rather than an all-powerful mayor.
- **Required simulation data:** One authority’s schedules, dependencies and action costs; new care/repair semantics require defined rules.
- **What makes it functional:** Actions consume real modeled time and capacity; counterfactual contribution uses a matched no-player continuation.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** One courier, two supported deliveries and one deadline on the Population clock. Avoid stitching monthly City costs onto minute-level actions.

#### 26. The City Is Your Escape Room

- **What the user experiences:** Escape an institutional deadlock by finding an order of legal, funded actions that makes a blocked project possible.
- **Why it is surprising:** Bureaucracy becomes a solvable temporal puzzle without arbitrary keys.
- **Required simulation data:** City project stages, budgets, capacities, policy preconditions and decision reasons.
- **What makes it functional:** Puzzle transitions run actual rules; victory is a recorded completion, with multiple solutions accepted if valid.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** One saved bottleneck case, three allowed capacity/budget choices and a turn limit. Reuse constraint inspection; reject unfunded or unsupported actions.

#### 27. Leave No Hero Behind

- **What the user experiences:** Stabilize a city, then gradually surrender controls until it continues functioning without your intervention.
- **Why it is surprising:** The win condition is becoming unnecessary.
- **Required simulation data:** Explicit player authority, supported policy lifecycle, stability predicates and a fixed evaluation horizon.
- **What makes it functional:** Score the hands-off continuation on held-out shocks; do not award permanence after a finite quiet interval.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** Use one persistent supported City plan, disable further commands after month twelve, and test a declared follow-up horizon. Compare against the same no-plan run.

### WebGPU experiences

#### 28. Walk Through an Uncertainty Tunnel

- **What the user experiences:** Walk along a street whose possible shapes split around you; choose a branch to enter its actual saved state.
- **Why it is surprising:** Uncertainty becomes navigable spatial structure.
- **Required simulation data:** Common-coordinate structure histories, same-time ensembles, lineage and complete/missing contributor counts.
- **What makes it functional:** GPU instances encode actual sampled states; picking resolves run/entity/time. Density is synthetic frequency, never forecast certainty.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** One street and eight replayed states rendered as offset slices. Canvas thumbnails provide equivalent selection; measure GPU cost before expanding.

#### 29. The Causal Loom

- **What the user experiences:** Pull one recorded decision thread and watch supported downstream dependencies illuminate across space and time.
- **Why it is surprising:** The city looks like woven consequences, with unknown gaps left visibly open.
- **Required simulation data:** Typed causal edges, event timestamps, positions and retained evidence; temporal correlation alone cannot supply edges.
- **What makes it functional:** GPU edge rendering preserves exact picking IDs and direction; unsupported causal connections stay absent and ablation contrasts remain separately labeled.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** One traced construction chain, fifty recorded edges, instanced lines and a textual traversal fallback. Verify every selectable edge against its source record.

#### 30. Two Histories, One Shadow

- **What the user experiences:** Move through two aligned futures while a shared shadow marks where they agree and split silhouettes show divergence.
- **Why it is surprising:** The player perceives continuity and disagreement at once without switching dashboards.
- **Required simulation data:** Matched geometry/entity IDs, recorded transforms, material states and exact synchronized checkpoints.
- **What makes it functional:** Dual rendering is read-only over pinned states; unmatched and unavailable geometry have distinct treatment, with no interpolated invented construction.
- **Cheapest useful prototype:** One block, two exact City checkpoints and a movable split lens. Start with ordinary render targets; add WebGPU instancing only if measured scale requires it.
